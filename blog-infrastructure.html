The Hidden Infrastructure Layer Powering the Global Experiences Economy

How the tours and activities industry is evolving from fragmented aggregation to unified infrastructure. Insights on what enterprise buyers should look for when building experiences into their platforms.

By Yaniv Green · 12 min read

Introduction

The tours and activities market has quietly become one of the fastest-growing segments in global travel. Yet while consumer-facing marketplaces have evolved rapidly, the underlying infrastructure powering this ecosystem has not.

Most travelers interact with sleek apps and polished booking flows. Behind the scenes, however, the experiences economy remains fragmented, unevenly connected, and full of operational blind spots. These gaps limit innovation, slow time-to-market, and prevent large brands from unlocking the full potential of experiences as a strategic growth lever.

Over the past few years, financial institutions, loyalty programs, super-apps, and travel-tech platforms have all converged on the same realization: experiences are no longer a "nice-to-have" category. They are a high-intent, high-engagement product layer that customers increasingly expect to find inside their everyday digital journeys.

Delivering experiences at scale, however, requires a fundamentally different type of infrastructure than selling flights or hotels.

1. The Tours & Activities Stack Is Far More Complex Than It Looks

Unlike flights or hotels, the experiences sector is not built on a small number of global suppliers with standardized systems. Instead, it is composed of:

  • Thousands of local operators
  • Multiple global aggregators
  • A mix of affiliate and merchant commercial models
  • Highly non-standardized content, pricing, availability, and cancellation logic

Most enterprise leaders underestimate how many layers must communicate seamlessly just to make a single "Book Now" button work.

A typical experiences ecosystem spans eight functional layers: local operators, reservation and ResTech systems, aggregators, affiliation networks, mapping engines, connectivity platforms, distribution enablers, and consumer-facing applications.

If even one of these layers is outdated, missing, or poorly connected, the entire value chain slows down.

2. Why Traditional Approaches Break Down for Modern Brands

Banks, fintechs, loyalty programs, mobility platforms, and large digital brands increasingly want to embed curated experiences directly into their customer journeys. Yet they consistently run into the same structural constraints:

  • Fragmented supply – No single supplier provides comprehensive global coverage across tours, attractions, events, and local experiences.
  • Integration overload – Every API follows different standards, creating content mismatches and long-term engineering debt.
  • Slow time-to-market – Building a scalable experiences offering in-house often takes 6–12 months.
  • Enterprise reliability gaps – Many travel suppliers were never designed for enterprise-grade volumes, SLAs, or real-time performance.
  • Limited personalization – Experiences are discovery-driven, but most platforms still rely on static lists rather than contextual recommendations.

For leaders responsible for loyalty, digital experience, or product innovation, these constraints make it increasingly difficult to capture the fast-growing demand for travel and lifestyle content.

3. The Industry Shift: From Aggregation to Infrastructure

The next frontier in the experiences economy is not more inventory.

It is orchestration.

Just as payments evolved from isolated gateways into unified rails, experiences are now moving toward a dedicated infrastructure layer that handles:

  • Multi-source aggregation
  • Content normalization and mapping
  • Distribution and commercial logic
  • White-label environments
  • AI-driven discovery and personalization
  • Enterprise onboarding and compliance
  • Reliability, monitoring, and scalability

This layer sits between raw supply and the brands that want to embed experiences into their ecosystems. It is rapidly becoming the connective tissue of the global experiences industry.

4. What Enterprise Buyers Should Look For

Across conversations with leaders in loyalty, travel-tech, fintech, and digital banking, the same evaluation criteria consistently emerge:

Breadth and Quality of Supply

Not only tours and activities, but also attractions, events, sports, and lifestyle experiences—across both merchant and affiliate models.

Normalization and Mapping Capabilities

The ability to unify disparate content sources into a single, consistent, high-quality catalog.

Distribution Flexibility

  • APIs for full product control
  • White-label marketplaces for speed
  • AI-powered discovery layers for engagement

Enterprise Readiness

Compliance support, multi-currency logic, analytics, uptime guarantees, SLAs, and scalable cloud architecture.

Personalization

Context-aware recommendations that reflect user intent rather than generic popularity-based lists.

Speed to Market

Activation in days or weeks—not quarters.

Increasingly, enterprises are no longer looking for "inventory." They are looking for infrastructure that compresses complexity into a single, reliable layer.

5. Experiences as a Strategic Differentiator

Experiences create stronger emotional engagement than almost any other digital product. When embedded correctly, they directly influence:

  • Customer retention and loyalty
  • Cross-sell and upsell performance
  • Card usage and wallet share in financial ecosystems
  • Daily engagement within super-apps
  • Brand differentiation in crowded markets

None of this is possible without the right technical foundation behind the scenes.

6. The Next Evolution: Intelligence-Driven Experiences

The future of this category will go beyond aggregation or distribution.

It will be driven by intelligence.

Users increasingly expect:

  • Intent-based search ("fun things to do with kids near me this weekend")
  • Contextual recommendations inside loyalty and lifestyle apps
  • Dynamic bundles built around travel itineraries or events
  • AI-guided discovery that adapts in real time

Delivering this requires historical data, behavioral signals, event-level context, and a unifying layer that understands both supply and user intent.

This is what will ultimately separate brands that merely offer "experiences" from brands that deliver true relevance.

Conclusion: A Quiet Transformation Already Underway

Tours and activities are no longer an isolated travel vertical. They are becoming a universal engagement layer across financial services, mobility, travel-tech, and loyalty ecosystems.

The real transformation, however, is happening beneath the surface - in the infrastructure layer where orchestration, intelligence, and connectivity define what consumers actually experience.

Leaders who understand this shift will be best positioned to build products that feel effortless to users and scalable for the business. Those who don't will continue to struggle with fragmented supply, long integration cycles, and outdated assumptions about what it takes to deliver modern experiences.

The gap between these two groups is widening fast.
